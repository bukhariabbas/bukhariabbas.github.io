Neurogenomics: regulation in context

How experience changes molecular regulation

My neurogenomics research examines how social experience, parental behavior, and infection relate to coordinated gene regulation. Across temporal, comparative, and single-cell studies, the recurring question is which molecular responses depend on time, cell type, and biological context.

Published contributions

As first author, I integrated brain RNA-seq, H3K27ac chromatin evidence, and inferred networks to study successive responses to territorial challenge in sticklebacks. The analysis connected different phases of the response to different biological processes. Bukhari et al., PLOS Genetics, 2017.

My first-author paternal-care study compared enduring, stage-specific, and cumulative transcriptional responses. It found parallels with mammalian parental responses and contrasting regulation during care and aggression. I contributed study design, analysis, and the first manuscript draft. Bukhari et al., Nature Communications, 2019 (study and published figure); analysis code.

Collaborative mouse, stickleback, and honeybee studies examined shared molecular responses and regulatory organization across species. I contributed data analysis to the 2014 study and coauthored the subsequent systems analysis. Rittschof et al., PNAS, 2014; Saul et al., Genes, Brain and Behavior, 2019.

My honeybee work brings a genotype-to-phenotype (G2P) perspective: connecting germline variation and genotype–expression associations with cellular regulons and behavioral differences. Explore the G2P evidence, including my public eQTL code and the distinction between inferred regulation and causal mechanism. I contributed methodology and manuscript review and editing to Traniello et al., Nature Ecology & Evolution, 2023.

I also performed regulatory-network analysis for a honeybee virus meta-analysis linking infection with maturation-associated brain programs, and coauthored a perspective on behavior-related networks. Traniello et al., Scientific Reports, 2020; Sinha et al., PNAS, 2020.

Questions for future brain-disease research

Human schizophrenia genetics and single-nucleus studies motivate asking where disease-associated genes converge on regulatory programs. These are findings by other investigators; my proposed contribution is comparative and cell-resolved analysis. Trubetskoy et al., Nature, 2022; Ruzicka et al., Science, 2024.

Studies of Alzheimer's disease similarly motivate comparisons among neuronal, glial, and vascular contexts, including vulnerability and resilience. Cain et al., Nature Neuroscience, 2023; Mathys et al., Nature, 2024; Yang et al., Nature, 2022.

Experimental immune-to-neural signaling and glioma–neuron interactions illustrate why tissue context matters. These separate studies establish findings within their respective model systems. Filiano et al., Nature, 2016; Venkatesh et al., Nature, 2019.

A shared analytical question

Across brain and tumor tissues, I ask whether a signal reflects changing cell abundance, regulation within cells, or both. Human disease applications would require suitable cohorts, attention to exposures and covariates, and experimental follow-up. My animal studies provide analytical foundations; they do not establish mechanisms of human psychiatric or neurodegenerative disease.
