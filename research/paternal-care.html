Testing competing models of biological transitions

First-author research examining how brain transcription changes across paternal care and how those responses relate to territorial aggression and parental care in other species.

My role
First author · Study design and analysis
Research output
Peer-reviewed study and public R code
Status
Nature Communications · 2019

The biological question

Does entering a parental state produce a persistent transcriptional shift, distinct changes at each stage, or an accumulation of responses? I tested these competing models in stickleback fathers and examined relationships with territorial aggression and parental responses across species.

My contribution

I contributed study design, performed data analysis, and wrote the first manuscript draft. The analytical work combined expression comparisons, explicit hypothesis tests, cross-species harmonization, and regulatory interpretation.

Key findings

The study resolved distinct temporal patterns during paternal care and connected some enduring responses in stickleback fathers with those observed in mammalian mothers. It also identified contrasting regulatory responses associated with parental care and territorial aggression.

Analytical approach and open code

Public R scripts document differential-expression analysis using edgeR and permutation-based empirical false-discovery-rate calculations. Comparative analyses tested shared biology while accounting for differences among species and study designs.

Why this work matters now

The transferable skill is disciplined model comparison. In disease research, I use the same habit of testing competing explanations rather than assuming that an appealing pattern represents a mechanism. Cross-study harmonization and sensitivity analysis remain central to my cohort-level work.

Context dependent regulation and disease hypotheses

Comparing enduring and stage-specific expression during paternal care trained me to treat biological transitions as competing models rather than a single case-control contrast. The study’s comparative and regulatory analyses also asked whether shared factors could be deployed differently across behavioral contexts. Its public R analysis code provides an inspectable record of this work.

A future application in brain disease would ask whether a program is stable, compensatory, or specific to a particular exposure or stage. Conservation across species would help prioritize hypotheses, while human genetic evidence, cell-resolved data, and experimental follow-up would determine disease relevance. The paternal-care findings are not presented as an animal model of psychiatric illness. Explore the broader neurogenomics trajectory.
