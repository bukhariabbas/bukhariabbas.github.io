Single-cell networks connecting genotype and behavior

A collaborative study connecting colony-level aggression, single-cell brain biology, and gene-regulatory architecture in honeybees.

Reading the research-plan figure

The illustration places cellular networks within a broader research trajectory: phenotype, time-dependent molecular change, cellular context, and candidate regulation. The honeybee study is one contribution to that trajectory, not the source of every assay and organism shown. Its single-cell perspective helps resolve where regulatory differences occur within heterogeneous brain tissue. My role in that study was methodology and manuscript review and editing, within a collaborative team.

The biological question

How does genetic variation associated with colony-level aggression relate to regulation within individual brains? Cell-type and brain-region context offer a way to connect genetic architecture with coordinated molecular responses.

My contribution

I contributed methodology and manuscript review and editing as a coauthor during my IGB postdoctoral period at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. This was a collaborative single-cell and regulatory-network study, not a project I claim to have led independently.

Key findings

Regulatory differences between soldiers and foragers were more pronounced in aggressive colonies, with coordinated network effects in sensory and integrative brain regions. The study showed how cellular context can illuminate phenotype-associated regulation beyond gene-expression differences alone.

Analytical perspective

Single-cell transcriptomic profiles and gene-regulatory networks connect variation in phenotype to cell-type-specific regulation. The network framework helps ask where and how molecular differences are organized within a heterogeneous tissue.

Why this work matters now

This experience strengthened my ability to interpret complex traits through cellular networks. In cancer, the analogous challenge is to distinguish regulatory programs within malignant and microenvironmental populations and connect them to testable disease mechanisms.

Connection to my future group

My proposed independent program extends this reasoning across pediatric tumors: compare states, investigate regulatory dependencies, and evaluate tissue and clinical context. It will begin with public or appropriately authorized data and validation partnerships scoped to explicit biological questions. See the Future Group page for the research aims, scientific independence, and plans for training a computationally anchored team.

What cellular networks add to the interpretation

In this collaborative study, colony context was associated more strongly with differences in regulatory organization than with differences in expression alone. That distinction matters: similar measurements of an individual gene can occur within differently organized networks. My contribution was methodology development and manuscript review and editing, within the team’s single-cell and regulatory-genomics investigation.

The broader application is to ask where a disease-associated program is organized and how its interpretation changes with cellular context. Human schizophrenia and Alzheimer’s studies motivate this question in neuronal and glial populations, but are separate from my insect research.
