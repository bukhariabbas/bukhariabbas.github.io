Scientific vision

My future group will investigate how regulatory programs create cellular diversity in pediatric tumors, and which of those programs expose vulnerabilities that can be tested experimentally. We will connect malignant cell states with their developmental and tissue contexts, then ask whether the resulting mechanisms explain differences in treatment response or risk across patients. The goal is to make the path from genomic observation to a useful experimental question more rigorous and more reproducible.

The program will be organized around biological questions, with computational biology and systems biology providing the means to answer them. Single-cell atlases will establish the cellular context; gene-regulatory networks will nominate mechanisms; functional-dependency evidence will guide perturbation studies; and clinically annotated cohorts will test relevance beyond the discovery dataset. A molecular pattern will advance because the evidence supports it, not simply because it can be detected in a large dataset.

Proposed research aim

Aim 1 Distinguish developmental regulation from tumor adaptation

Which cellular programs recur across patients, and which resemble normal developmental regulation rather than a tumor-specific response? My group will compare malignant, immune, and stromal populations with relevant normal references, starting with rhabdomyosarcoma and extending to other pediatric tumors where suitable data exist. Published RMS studies provide a foundation for investigating developmental hierarchies and therapy-associated states. Wei et al., Nature Cancer, 2022; Danielli et al., Nature Communications, 2024.

We will identify recurrent expression programs and test their stability in patients held out from discovery. Comparisons will account for molecular subtype, proliferation, genomic dosage, stress, and tissue composition. Similarity to a developmental reference will motivate a hypothesis, not establish a cell of origin. Where a relationship does not generalize, we will examine tumor-specific and technical explanations instead of imposing a shared trajectory.

The first output will be a reproducible reference of cellular programs with explicit annotations, uncertainty, and evidence for competing interpretations. This will identify which states warrant mechanistic investigation and which remain too unstable or context-dependent to prioritize.

Proposed research aim

Aim 2 Identify regulators with testable therapeutic dependencies

Which regulators help maintain a tumor state, and does interfering with them create a selective vulnerability? We will integrate transcription-factor evidence and consensus regulatory networks with functional-dependency and pharmacologic resources. Candidate selection will consider disease context, model suitability, generic essentiality, and contradictory evidence rather than rely on expression alone.

With experimental cancer biologists, we will co-design perturbation comparisons that measure both the predicted molecular program and relevant phenotypes, including viability and treatment response. A candidate will be deprioritized when its evidence is explained by generic proliferation, or when an effective perturbation fails to alter the predicted state. This connects computational analysis to experiments that can support or reject a mechanism.

Deliverables will include ranked regulator–program hypotheses, traceable evidence summaries, and model-specific validation plans. Computational nomination and experimental target validation will remain distinct stages.

Proposed research aim

Aim 3 Test cellular programs in tissue and clinical context

Do cellular states and their surrounding immune or stromal environments explain response or prognosis beyond established clinical predictors? My group will connect single-cell discoveries to clinically annotated cohorts, separating changes in cell abundance from changes in expression within cells. Models will be matched to the number of patients and events, evaluated against simpler clinical baselines, and assessed for calibration, discrimination, and reproducibility.

Independent cohorts will be used when available. Spatial studies with appropriate partners would test whether inferred relationships correspond to actual tissue neighborhoods. We will not advance a signature as a biomarker candidate if it fails to add reproducible information. Prognostic association alone will not be interpreted as evidence that a treatment benefits a patient.

The output will be evaluated response and risk hypotheses, with clear limits on where they apply. The three aims are related but not interdependent: each can deliver useful findings with the data and validation resources available to it.

Scientific independence

My independent direction is the study of how regulatory programs connect cellular plasticity, tissue context, and therapeutic vulnerability across pediatric cancers. It brings together the comparative reasoning developed in my doctoral research, the cellular and network perspective of my postdoctoral work, and my current computational leadership in pediatric oncology. The scientific continuity is a question about regulation and phenotype, not dependence on a single assay, tumor collection, or analytical method.

I bring experience in framing biological questions, designing comparisons, developing reproducible analyses, and interpreting results in the presence of competing explanations. My first-author work integrated temporal transcription and chromatin evidence and tested models of behavioral transitions. My current role connects single-cell program discovery with regulatory interpretation and cohort analysis, while SigClust demonstrates my contribution to reusable research software. These foundations position me to define biological questions, set analytical standards, and direct collaborative projects from discovery through validation.

The future group's intellectual contribution will be its questions, comparative framework, software, and criteria for deciding which candidate mechanisms deserve further study. My current NCI work is collaborative; the proposed program does not presume exclusive ownership of shared data or automatic transfer of restricted resources. Initial projects will use public or appropriately authorized datasets, and any continuation involving institutional data will require the relevant agreements.

A coherent scientific trajectory

My doctoral research examined how changing social experience is reflected in coordinated molecular responses. Time-resolved RNA-seq, H3K27ac chromatin evidence, and regulatory-network analysis helped connect behavioral variation to candidate mechanisms. Postdoctoral comparative work and methodological contributions to a single-cell honeybee brain study extended this reasoning across species and cell types.

At NCI, in Dr. Javed Khan's laboratory, I apply these principles to pediatric tumors: which programs recur across patients, what regulates them, and what evidence would justify a therapeutic experiment? The systems have changed, but the central challenge is consistent: distinguish reproducible biological organization from composition, context, and technical variation. My future group will build on this trajectory by taking responsibility for an integrated program of discovery, critical evaluation, and collaborative validation.

A connected direction in brain genomic medicine

My immediate independent program is anchored in pediatric cancer, with a connected longer-term interest in neuronal, glial, immune, and vascular contexts in brain biology. My earlier neurogenomics research examined experience-dependent regulation, comparative responses, and cellular networks. Current tumor analyses extend that reasoning to the distinction between intrinsic programs and the surrounding tissue environment.

Through appropriate collaborations, I would investigate which regulatory programs accompany adaptive plasticity, vulnerability, or resilience in psychiatric and neurodegenerative research. The shared strategy is to resolve cell states, test competing explanations, and connect candidate mechanisms to independent evidence. Mechanisms and cell identities would be established within each system, not assumed to be interchangeable between tumors and the brain.

The expanded neurogenomics portfolio details my published contributions and the primary literature motivating these proposed applications. This is a future collaborative direction, not a claim of completed human brain-disease studies or an established second laboratory program.

Building the group and its research culture

I envision a computationally anchored group working closely with experimental cancer biologists and clinical investigators. Early projects will be scoped around accessible datasets and explicit validation questions. Partnerships will be developed around suitable model systems, specimens, and readouts; spatial and perturbational studies will depend on the relevant expertise and resources.

I will mentor researchers through written study plans, reproducible R/Python workflows, code review, and biological interpretation. Trainees should learn not only how to execute an analysis, but how to recognize a weak comparison, explain uncertainty, and design the next test. Statistical and machine-learning approaches will be compared with simpler baselines. AI-assisted implementation will remain subject to human control of scientific questions, data access, model selection, interpretation, and release decisions.

Initial institutional or foundation support would establish feasibility before broader external funding is pursued. Success will mean reusable cellular references and software, well-supported mechanistic candidates, and collaborative studies that advance pediatric therapeutic discovery. Clinical benefit is the long-term motivation, not a claim that the proposed analyses already improve patient outcomes.
